# Lesson: The Busy Builders: How Beavers Shape Their Environment

**Grade:** 4
**Subject:** English Language Arts (Informational Reading)
**Unit:** Nature's Engineers: How Animals Shape Their Environments
**Time:** 45 minutes

---

## 1. Standards (Common Core State Standards, ELA, Grade 4)

- **RI.4.1:** Refer to details and examples in a text when explaining what the text says explicitly and when drawing inferences from the text.
- **RI.4.2:** Determine the main idea of a text and explain how it is supported by key details; summarize the text.
- **RI.4.3:** Explain events, procedures, ideas, or concepts in a historical, scientific, or technical text, including what happened and why, based on specific information in the text.
- **RI.4.4:** Determine the meaning of general academic and domain-specific words and phrases in a text relevant to a *grade 4 topic or subject area*.
- **L.4.4.a:** Use context (e.g., definitions, examples, or restatements in text) as a clue to the meaning of a word or phrase.
- **SL.4.1:** Engage effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on *grade 4 topics and texts*, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly.

---

## 2. Learning Objective

**I can** explain how beavers change their environment by building dams and lodges, and tell how those changes help other plants and animals.

---

## 3. Materials

- Copies of the reading passage "The Busy Builders" (one per student)
- Vocabulary cards with the five key words and student-friendly definitions (one set per pair)
- Projector or chart paper with the passage's first paragraph displayed (for modeling)
- Sticky notes (two colors) or index cards
- Highlighters or colored pencils
- Exit ticket slips (one per student)
- Timer

## 4. Reading Passage

### The Busy Builders

Beavers are some of the hardest-working animals in North America. They are known as "nature's engineers" because the homes and structures they build can change the land around them for many years.

A beaver's most famous project is the dam. Beavers usually build dams across slow-moving streams. They start by gnawing down small trees with their strong, orange front teeth. Then they drag branches, logs, mud, and stones into the water. They pack everything tightly together so that the water cannot easily flow through. Over time, the dam grows taller and wider, and the water behind it rises.

Why do beavers go to all this trouble? A deeper pond is a safer place to live. Water helps protect beavers from predators such as wolves, coyotes, and bears. The pond also gives beavers a place to store food. Beavers cannot hibernate, so they stash tree branches in the mud at the bottom of the pond. They can swim to this underwater pantry and eat all winter long.

Inside the pond, beavers build a lodge. A lodge is a dome-shaped home made of sticks and mud. Its entrance is hidden underwater, so the family can swim in and out safely. Inside, there is a dry resting spot above the water line where the beavers sleep and raise their young.

The changes beavers make do not stop with their own homes. When the pond spreads across a valley, it creates a wetland. Many plants, such as cattails and water lilies, grow well in the shallow, soggy soil. Frogs, ducks, fish, and turtles move in to feed and hide among the plants. Dragonflies lay their eggs in the still water, and moose may wade in to eat the tender water plants. Even birds such as herons hunt for fish in the pond.

Some people once thought beavers caused problems, but scientists now understand that these builders can help the land. Ponds slow down rushing water, which can reduce flooding downstream. Wetlands also hold water during dry months, and they can filter out dirt before it reaches rivers. In this way, a single beaver family can transform a quiet stream into a busy home for many living things.

*(Word count: approximately 350)*

## 5. Key Vocabulary

| Word | Student-Friendly Definition |
|---|---|
| **dam** | A wall built across a stream or river to hold back water. |
| **lodge** | A beaver's home, shaped like a dome and made of sticks and mud, with an entrance under water. |
| **predator** | An animal that hunts and eats other animals to survive. |
| **wetland** | A soggy area of land, like a marsh or pond edge, where the ground is wet for most of the year. |
| **transform** | To change something so much that it looks or works in a new way. |

## 6. Lesson Procedure (45 minutes)

### Opening (5 minutes)
1. Write the title "Nature's Engineers" on the board. Ask: *"What does an engineer do?"* Take three or four quick answers (build, design, solve problems).
2. Show a photo of a beaver dam. Ask: *"What do you notice? What might this animal be trying to do?"*
3. Read the "I can" objective aloud. Explain: *"Today you will read about beavers and find out how one animal can change a whole area."*

### Vocabulary (7 minutes)
1. Give each pair a set of vocabulary cards. Read each word aloud and have students repeat it.
2. Model using context clues with the word **transform** from the passage's final paragraph: *"Look at the sentence before and after 'transform.' The text says a family can turn a quiet stream into a busy home. What clues help you figure out what 'transform' means?"*
3. Pairs discuss one more word using context clues. Students note the page and sentence where each word appears on the back of its card.

### Reading (12 minutes)
1. Students read the passage silently. Tell them to underline the **main idea** of each paragraph with a light pencil.
2. Students use a sticky note to mark one detail about how beavers change the land and a second sticky note to mark one detail about how the changes help other living things.
3. Early finishers reread the paragraph about the pond and wetland and draw a quick sketch of the changed landscape.

### Discussion (10 minutes)
1. Pairs discuss: *"How does a dam change a stream? Use a detail from the text to support your answer."*
2. Model the sentence frame: *"The text says ___. This shows that beavers ___."*
3. Bring the class together. Ask: *"Why does the author say beavers are 'nature's engineers'?"* Record key ideas on chart paper.

### Practice (8 minutes)
1. Students write a short summary of the passage (three to four sentences) that includes the main idea and two key details.
2. Partners exchange summaries and check that each includes a main idea and supporting details. Partners give one specific compliment and one suggestion.

### Closing (3 minutes)
1. Ask two or three students to share one sentence from their summary.
2. Preview the exit ticket and collect it as students leave.

## 7. Text-Dependent Comprehension Questions

1. **According to the passage, what are two reasons beavers build dams? (RI.4.1)**
   *Answer key:* Beavers build dams to create a deeper pond that protects them from predators, and to create a place to store food (tree branches) for winter. Beavers also build ponds that give them a safe place to live. (Accept any two of these.)

2. **The passage says a beaver's lodge has an entrance hidden underwater. Why would this be helpful to a beaver family? (RI.4.3)**
   *Answer key:* The underwater entrance keeps the family safe from predators, because the predators cannot easily reach them inside. The family can swim in and out without being seen. (Accept reasoning that links the entrance to safety.)

3. **What is the main idea of the section titled "The changes beavers make do not stop with their own homes"? Support your answer with two details from the text. (RI.4.2)**
   *Answer key:* Main idea: When beavers create ponds, they change the land and create homes for many other plants and animals. Supporting details: Cattails and water lilies grow in the shallow soil; frogs, ducks, fish, and turtles move in; dragonflies lay eggs; moose eat water plants; herons hunt fish. (Accept any two accurate details.)

4. **The author uses the word "transform" in the last paragraph. Using context clues from the sentence, explain what "transform" means. (RI.4.4, L.4.4.a)**
   *Answer key:* "Transform" means to change something so much that it works or looks different. The sentence says a beaver family can turn a quiet stream into a busy home for many living things, which shows that the word describes a big change. (Accept any answer that uses the context to explain a major change.)

---

## 8. Exit Ticket

On an index card or slip, answer in complete sentences:

1. Name one way a beaver dam changes the environment.
2. Explain how this change helps at least one other plant or animal. Use a detail from the passage.

**Scoring guide (4 points):**
- **4:** Names a specific change and explains a clear, text-based benefit for another living thing, in complete sentences.
- **3:** Names a change and gives a benefit with minor gaps in detail or explanation.
- **2:** Names a change or a benefit, but not both fully.
- **1:** Response is unclear, off-topic, or missing.

## 9. Differentiation

### For English Learners
- **Preview vocabulary with visuals.** Show a photo or simple drawing for each of the five words (dam, lodge, predator, wetland, transform) before reading. Pair each with a gesture, such as stacking hands for *dam* or a dome shape for *lodge*.
- **Provide a bilingual word bank** for the five key words and for *beaver*, *pond*, *food*, and *safe*.
- **Use sentence frames** in discussion and writing: *"A beaver builds a ___ to ___."* *"The pond helps ___ because ___."*
- **Allow oral responses.** Students may say their answers to a partner or the teacher before writing them.
- **Pre-teach the idiom "nature's engineers"** by explaining that the phrase means beavers solve problems and build structures in nature, like engineers do.

### For Students Who Need More Reading Support
- **Provide a chunked or highlighted copy** of the passage, with one paragraph per section and key sentences in bold.
- **Read the passage aloud** or use text-to-speech while students follow along with the text.
- **Use a graphic organizer** with three columns: *What beavers build / What changes / Who benefits*. Students complete it during reading.
- **Shorten the practice task** to a two-sentence summary with one detail.
- **Offer a question card** for the text-dependent questions that points to the paragraph where each answer appears.
- **Extend for advanced readers:** Ask them to compare the beaver's changes to a change made by a different animal, such as a prairie dog or a termite, and to note whether the changes are helpful or harmful.
