1. Unit overview

Essential question: How can one animal change a whole environment, and is that change always good?

By the end of the unit, students understand that:

  • Some animals change their habitat so much that other living things come to depend on the change. Scientists call them ecosystem engineers.
  • Engineers work in different ways: with water (beavers), with soil (gopher tortoises), and with rock and coral (parrotfish).
  • Whether an engineer helps or harms depends on the place and the other living things that share it.

Students will be able to: explain a chain of causes and effects using details from a text (RI.4.3, RI.4.1); integrate information from two texts on the same topic (RI.4.9); determine word meanings from context and word parts (L.4.4a, L.4.4b); and write an explanatory piece with facts and details from more than one text (W.4.2, W.4.9b).

Why this topic

It is a science topic with a strong cause-and-effect structure, which suits RI.4.3. It builds a body of connected knowledge rather than a list of animal facts, so every text makes the next one easier to read. It also lets students in The Bahamas see their own reefs and beaches in the content, through the parrotfish texts.

4. Five fully written lessons

Every lesson follows the same routine so that students' attention goes to the text, not the procedure. From Lesson 2 on, lessons open with a three-question retrieval warm-up on earlier texts. Each lesson names the language strands of Scarborough's Reading Rope it builds.

Lesson 1 · What changed, and why? (Text 1, first read)

Objective: I can explain how a beaver dam changes a stream, using a chain of causes and effects from the text. Standards: RI.4.3, RI.4.1. Rope strands: background knowledge, verbal reasoning.

Materials: Text 1 with numbered paragraphs; cause-and-effect chain organizer (four linked boxes); chart paper.

Launch · 8 min
Show the essential question. Ask students to think of a time they changed a place (built a fort, dug a hole at the beach). "What changed because of what you did? Who else noticed?" Partners share; take three answers.
"Today we meet an animal that builds something so big it changes a whole stream, and the lives of many other animals."
First read · 12 min
Read paragraphs 1–2 aloud while students follow. Students read paragraphs 3–6 with a partner, alternating paragraphs. Purpose: "What changes in this place, from the beginning to the end?"
Model · 10 min
Think aloud with paragraph 2, filling the first two boxes of the chain.
"The text says beavers 'pack them together with mud and stones.' That makes a dam. What happens because of the dam? 'The water backs up behind the dam and spreads out into a pond.' So box one is dam, box two is pond. The word 'behind' tells me the pond is caused by the dam."
Guided practice · 15 min
Partners complete boxes three and four from paragraph 4, then write the paragraph number beside each box. Circulate and ask: "Which words in the text tell you this happened because of the box before it?"
Discuss · 8 min
Build one class chain on chart paper. Ask: "Which change surprised you most? Why does the author wait until paragraph 5 to give these animals a name?"
Check · 7 min
On their own: "Write two links of the chain in sentences, with a paragraph number for each." This previews the cause-and-effect items on the end-of-unit assessment.

Supports: the text is available to listen to; the organizer has sentence starters ("Because ___, ___."); students may give the check orally. Extension: add a fifth box: who could be harmed by the pond?

Look-fors: each link is a cause, not just the next event in the text; each has a paragraph reference.

Lesson 2 · Words that carry the science (Text 1, second read)

Objective: I can figure out the meaning of science words using clues in the sentence and parts of the word. Standards: RI.4.4, L.4.4a, L.4.4b. Rope strands: vocabulary, language structures.

Retrieval · 5 min
No notes: (1) What do beavers build across a stream? (2) What forms behind it? (3) Name one animal that comes to the pond, and why.
Teach · 12 min
Two kinds of clues. Context: "predators such as coyotes and bears" (an example clue). Word parts: eco- (home, environment) in "ecosystem." Model both on the board.
"The text doesn't define 'predators,' but 'such as' tells me examples are coming. Coyotes and bears both hunt. So a predator is probably an animal that hunts other animals."
Practice · 18 min
Partners work on four words: lodge (¶3), wetland (¶4), habitat (¶4), fortress (¶3). For each: the clue, the kind of clue, and a meaning in their own words. Fortress is the challenge word: a metaphor. "Why would the author call a pond a fortress?"
Reread · 12 min
Students reread the whole text independently, adding any new links to their Lesson 1 chain now that the words are clearer.
Check · 8 min
Explain two of the words and name the clue that helped. This previews the vocabulary items on the assessment.

Supports: picture cards for lodge and wetland; bilingual glossary where students need one. The vocabulary routine continues in WS3.

Lesson 3 · Two engineers, one pattern (Text 2)

Objective: I can explain how two animals change their habitats in similar and different ways, using details from both texts. Standards: RI.4.9, RI.4.1. Rope strands: background knowledge, verbal reasoning.

Retrieval · 5 min
(1) What is an ecosystem engineer? (2) What is a predator? (3) Why is the beaver pond safe for beavers?
Read · 15 min
Students read Text 2 in pairs. Purpose: "How is the gopher tortoise like the beaver? How is it different?" Students mark S (same) and D (different) in the margin.
Organize · 15 min
Two-column chart: Beaver | Gopher tortoise, with rows for what it builds, what it changes, who else benefits, why it matters. Every cell needs a paragraph number from the right text.
Discuss · 12 min
Small groups: "The last paragraph says the beaver works with water and the tortoise works with sand. What else could an engineer work with?" Groups share; record predictions for later texts.
Check · 8 min
Write two sentences: one similarity and one difference, each with evidence from both texts. Previews the two-text item on the assessment.

Language support: frames for comparing: "Both ___ and ___ ___." "The beaver ___, but the gopher tortoise ___."

Lesson 4 · Explaining a process (Text 3)

Objective: I can explain the steps in a process and show them in a labeled diagram. Standards: RI.4.3, RI.4.7. Rope strands: background knowledge, language structures.

Retrieval · 5 min
(1) What does "keystone species" mean? (2) What does a beaver build with? (3) What does a gopher tortoise build with?
Read · 15 min
Teacher reads paragraphs 1–2 aloud. Students read 3–5 independently, underlining each step in how sand is made.
Diagram · 18 min
Students draw a four-step diagram (scrape, grind, digest, sand) with a label and a caption for each step taken from the text. Model the first caption: "Step 1: The parrotfish scrapes algae off coral with its beak (¶2)."
Discuss · 12 min
"Paragraph 4 describes a second way parrotfish change the reef. Is that part of the sand process or a different effect? How do you know?" This separates a process from a related cause and effect.
Check · 8 min
Explain in three sentences how a parrotfish makes sand, using sequence words.

Connection: invite students who have been to a Bahamian beach to describe the sand. The next lesson's story is set at Taino Beach.

Lesson 5 · The same science in a story (Text 4, with Text 3)

Objective: I can describe how a character's thinking changes, using details from the story, and explain how the story and the informational text present the same science. Standards: RL.4.3; connects to RI.4.9 through Text 3. Rope strands: verbal reasoning (inference), literacy knowledge (genre).

Retrieval · 5 min
(1) What do parrotfish eat? (2) What happens to the rock they swallow? (3) How do parrotfish help coral?
Read · 12 min
Read the story aloud once for enjoyment. Students reread in pairs, marking where Kiara's opinion of the fish changes.
Infer · 13 min
"The story never says the white cloud is sand coming out of the fish. How do we know?" Students list clues (Kiara's wrinkled nose, "Did it just...," her uncle's laugh). Link to the science they learned in Lesson 4.
Compare · 15 min
Two-column chart: what "The Sand Makers" tells us | how "The Crunching Sound" shows the same thing. Discuss: "Which one helped you understand better? Which one made you care more?"
Check · 10 min
"How does Kiara's thinking about the parrotfish change? Use two details from the story." This previews the literary-response skill used again in Grade 5 (WS2 text 6).

Lessons 6–10 in brief

  • Lesson 6 (Text 5): teacher-supported read in three chunks; students track the author's claim and two reasons (RI.4.8). Check: complete "Whether an engineer helps or harms depends on ___, because ___."
  • Lesson 7 (all texts): groups build a synthesis chart across the four engineers: what it builds, who benefits, who could be harmed. Structured discussion with assigned roles (SL.4.1).
  • Lesson 8: introduce the culminating task and rubric. Students choose two engineers and plan with a two-text evidence organizer (W.4.2a, W.4.9b).
  • Lesson 9: model an introduction and one body paragraph; students draft, using linking words (for example, also, because, another, however).
  • Lesson 10: partner feedback with the rubric; revise; share one paragraph aloud.

5. Culminating task

Prompt. You have read about animals that change their environments. Choose two of them. Write an explanation that tells how each animal changes its environment and how those changes affect other living things. Use facts and details from at least two of the texts. End with a conclusion about what your two animals show about ecosystem engineers.
