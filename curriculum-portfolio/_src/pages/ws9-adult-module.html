1. Challenge

New teachers improve fastest when someone who has watched them teach talks with them about it. Many school leaders and mentors were never taught how to hold that conversation. It often turns into a verdict or a list, and the teacher leaves with nothing they own. Research reviews link well-structured induction and mentoring to beginning teachers' practice and retention (Ingersoll & Strong, 2011), and coaching that is specific and sustained has measurable effects on instruction (Kraft, Blazar & Hogan, 2018). My doctoral research on leadership behaviors and teacher retention is the reason I chose this topic.

2. Context and constraints

  • Learners: busy adults who already give feedback and have opinions about it. Time is the main constraint: about 40 minutes online, spread over a week, plus one 90-minute live session.
  • Delivery: self-paced online lessons in any common LMS, then a live session in person or on video.
  • Must not: require special software, collect personal data, or depend on video that has to be filmed.

3. Analysis

Performance goal and action map

The goal is a behavior: mentors hold feedback conversations that are specific, two-way and end with one agreed next step. Action mapping (Moore, 2017) listed what mentors must do to reach it, then asked, for each action, whether the gap is knowledge, skill or environment. Only knowledge and skill gaps get course content. The environment gap (no protected time to meet) goes in the guidance for school leaders.

Audience

Learners range from first-time mentors to principals with years of experience. Both groups give feedback already, so the course respects what they know: it opens by asking for their own experience, and the live session starts from their real cases (Knowles, Holton & Swanson, 2015). Most will take the online lessons on a laptop between other tasks, so each lesson is short and can be left and resumed.

4. Learning objectives

By the end of the course, mentors will be able to:

  1. Identify the verdict, list and monologue patterns in a feedback conversation.
  2. Rewrite a judgment as a piece of observable evidence.
  3. Choose mentor responses that keep a conversation specific and two-way, and explain why.
  4. Plan a conversation with a real teacher using the four moves: evidence, effect, question, next step.
  5. Hold that conversation in a rehearsal and give a partner feedback on it using the same moves.

Iteration in design

Before: the first course map had a fourth online lesson summarizing research on feedback. After: action mapping showed that no on-the-job action depended on it, so it became two sentences in Lesson 1, and the time went to the scenario. Before: the planner asked mentors to write the whole conversation as a script. After: a script encourages a monologue, so the planner now asks for evidence, effect and one question, and leaves the next step open for the teacher to shape.

8. Accessibility and UDL

  • Every interaction works by keyboard; buttons are real buttons and fields have visible labels.
  • The worked example is text, so it can be read, heard with the site's Listen reader, or printed. No video needs captions.
  • Feedback never relies on color alone; it always says "Correct" or "Not yet" in words.
  • Learners can type or speak their plan in the live session; the planner accepts short notes.
  • Checked against WCAG 2.2 AA success criteria as a reference. No conformance claim is made.

10. Usability test plan

  • Participants: five mentors or school leaders, including at least one who uses a screen reader or keyboard only, if available.
  • Tasks: start the course and find Lesson 2; complete the scenario; pass the knowledge check; download the plan; find the live session details.
  • Measures: task success, time on task, points of hesitation, and a short ease-of-use rating.
  • Method: think-aloud on video call, 30 minutes each. Fix anything that blocks two or more participants before launch.

11. Pre-launch QA checklist

  • Every objective is practiced and assessed (see course map).
  • All feedback text is written for every option, including correct ones.
  • Scenario paths all reach an ending; no dead ends.
  • Keyboard-only pass: every control reachable, visible focus, logical order.
  • Screen-reader pass: headings in order, buttons announced, live feedback read aloud.
  • Readability: plain language, sentences under 25 words where possible.
  • Links and downloads work; planner opens on phone and laptop.
  • LMS completion rules tested with a test learner account.
  • Names and situations checked for bias and cultural fit.

13. Facilitator guide: live session (90 minutes)

Before the session: confirm all participants passed the knowledge check and have a plan. Print the rewrite cards and the observation checklist (below). For an online session, set up breakout rooms for pairs and paste the cards into a shared document.

TimeSegmentWhat the facilitator does
0–10Welcome and purposeState the goal: "Today you'll rehearse the conversation you planned, so the real one goes better." Ask each person for one word that describes their last feedback conversation.
10–25Rewrite drillHand out eight judgment cards ("The lesson dragged," "They were off task"). Pairs rewrite each as evidence. Debrief: "What did you need to know to rewrite it?" Point out that you can only give evidence you collected.
25–35ModelFacilitator and a volunteer run a ten-minute version of a conversation. Participants mark each move on the checklist as they hear it.
35–70Paired rehearsalIn pairs, A plays the mentor using their own plan; B plays the teacher. Ten minutes, then five minutes of feedback using the four moves. Swap. Remind mentors to stay silent for at least five seconds after their question.
70–80DebriefAsk: "Where did the conversation turn two-way? What made that happen?" Collect patterns on a chart.
80–90CommitEach person writes the date of their real conversation and what they will record for the 30-day check-in. Complete the reaction survey.

Guidance note for school leaders

The course can teach the conversation, but it cannot create the time for it. Schedule mentor and new teacher meetings within two days of each observation, and protect that time. Without it, the skill has nowhere to be used.

14. What I would do next

Run the usability test with five mentors, revise, then pilot with one cohort and collect Level 1–3 data against the targets above. If Level 3 shows mentors skip the wait time after their question, add a short audio example of what a five-second pause sounds like in practice.
