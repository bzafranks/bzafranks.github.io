Adapting a Unit With a Community, Then Supporting Teachers

A unit written far from a classroom only becomes that classroom's unit when the people around it shape it. This sample shows the process I would use to adapt Nature's Engineers with a Grand Bahama school community, and the workshop that helps teachers teach it.

What is real and what is illustrative. The process, tools, adapted lesson and workshop are complete. No consultation has taken place, so the feedback log uses invented, clearly labeled examples to show how feedback becomes decisions. No community member, organization or quotation is real.

1. Principles

  • Ask before writing. The community shapes what is adapted, not just how it is worded.
  • Record every input and every decision. People should be able to see what happened to what they said.
  • Local knowledge is credited, never borrowed silently. Anyone who contributes knowledge is asked how they want to be acknowledged, and nothing is published without their agreement.
  • Teachers decide what works in their classrooms. The workshop ends with their plan, not mine.

3. Consultation guide

Questions for teachers

  1. Which local places or animals would your students recognize right away?
  2. What topics need care with your students right now? (For example, many Grand Bahama families were affected by Hurricane Dorian in 2019; a story about a storm, like "Big Claw's Burrow," may need a check with teachers first.)
  3. Which parts of the unit would be hardest to teach in your timetable?
  4. What would make the culminating writing task feel worth doing for your students?

Questions for local experts

  1. Which animals here change the places they live in ways other living things depend on?
  2. What have you seen change on the reef, beach or coast over the years?
  3. Is there anything in our texts that is wrong or would sound wrong to people here?
  4. Would you be willing to talk with students, and how would you like to be credited?

Questions for families (take-home card)

  1. What is a place near home where you have watched animals?
  2. Is there a story, saying or memory in your family about the sea, the land or animals that you would like to share?

Recording: notes are written during the conversation, read back to the speaker for accuracy, and stored with the date and the speaker's role (not name, unless they choose to be named).

5. Adapted lesson: Lesson 0, "Engineers on Our Island"

Grade 4 · 60 minutes · inserted before Lesson 1

Objective: I can describe how an animal I know changes the place it lives, and who else is affected, using what I observe and what others tell me. Standards: SL.4.1, SL.4.4, W.4.8 (gather information from experience).

Launch · 10 min
"Places I know" map: students mark beach, reef, yard, creek or pine forest areas they know on a simple island outline. Ask: "What animals have you seen there? What do they do to the place?"
Observe · 15 min
Option A (outdoors): look for crab holes, ant mounds or bird nests on or near the school grounds and sketch them. Option B (indoors): photos of a land crab burrow and parrotfish on a reef. Students note what the animal built and what changed around it.
Listen · 15 min
A local expert's visit or recorded talk, or a family story collected with the take-home card, shared with permission. Students write one thing they learned and one question.
Connect · 15 min
Introduce the unit's essential question. "Which of our island animals might be an engineer? What would we need to find out?" Students record predictions to revisit after Text 1.
Close · 5 min
Exit: "One animal on our island that changes its home is ___. I think so because ___."

Grade 5 classes can use "Rebuilding the Reef" (WS2 text 6) in the same way, with a local restoration or conservation worker as the visitor.

6. Teacher workshop: implementing the unit (90 minutes)

Outcomes

By the end, teachers can explain how the unit builds knowledge, run the cause-and-effect chain routine, recognize strong and weak student explanations, and leave with a plan for their first week.

Follow-up coaching

Two short cycles in the first three weeks. Each cycle: the coach observes 20 minutes of a unit lesson focused on the teacher's chosen look-for, then holds a feedback conversation within two days using the four moves from WS9 (evidence, effect, question, next step). The teacher chooses the next step; the coach returns to look for it.

Evaluating the workshop

Reaction survey at the end of the session; look-for observations in coaching visits (are routines in place?); and a sample of student chains and final explanations reviewed by teachers together after the unit.
