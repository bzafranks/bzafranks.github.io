From Sentence to Essay

An eight-session sequence. Students read two texts that disagree in important ways, build strong sentences about them, turn the sentences into paragraphs, and write an opinion essay that uses evidence from both.

1. Why sentence-level work first

Many upper-elementary students can state an opinion but cannot yet write the sentences an argument needs: a reason joined to its evidence, a concession joined to a response. If those sentences are not secure, a five-paragraph organizer only produces five weak paragraphs. So this sequence builds the sentences first, using content students already understand from their reading, and only then asks for paragraphs and an essay. Sentence combining in particular has a solid research base for improving the quality of students' writing (Graham & Perin, 2007). The techniques below are described in my own words and are not taken from any commercial program.

2. The paired texts

"When Engineers Cause Trouble" shows beavers harming forests where they never belonged. "Welcome Back, Beavers?" describes beavers returning to Britain, where they once lived, with real benefits and real objections. Together they give students two sides and a reason the answer might differ by place.

Session 3 · Sentences that join a claim to its reason

Objective: I can write sentences that connect an idea to its reason or to an opposing idea, using evidence from the texts. Standards: L.5.1a, W.5.1c.

Model · 10 min
Show one idea from Text B and three ways to continue it, each joined by a different conjunction:
Beavers' ponds held back large amounts of water
…because the dams slowed the stream. (gives a cause)
…although some farmers worry about flooded fields. (adds a contrast)
…so towns downstream may flood less. (gives a result)
"Each joining word does a different job. Because explains why. Although admits another side. So tells what happens next."
Guided · 15 min
Pairs complete three stems with all three conjunctions, using only facts from the texts:
1. Beavers in Tierra del Fuego have damaged forests…
2. Some people want beavers back in Britain…
3. Managing beavers takes time and money…
Partners check: "Is everything after the joining word true according to the text?"
Independent · 15 min
Students write their own opinion as a sentence, then extend it three ways. These become the raw material for Sessions 5 and 6.
Check · 5 min
Exit: one although sentence about beavers that is accurate to the texts.

Common error and response: students write "although" sentences where both parts agree ("Although beavers help, they make wetlands"). Ask: "Does the second part push against the first? What does someone on the other side say?"

Session 4 · Combining and expanding

Objective: I can combine short sentences and expand a plain sentence so my evidence is clear and precise. Standard: L.5.3a.

Combine · 15 min
Students combine sets drawn from the texts, then compare versions:
Beavers were released in Scotland. It was 2009. Scientists studied them carefully.
→ In 2009, beavers were released in Scotland, where scientists studied them carefully.
Discuss: which words did you drop? Which did you keep? Is anything lost?
Expand · 15 min
Start with a kernel: "Beavers help." Students add answers to help what?, how? and according to whom?, one at a time:
Beavers help. → Beaver ponds help towns. → Beaver ponds may help towns downstream by slowing water during heavy rain. → According to a study in southwest England, beaver ponds slowed the water flowing out of the site during heavy rain, which could help towns downstream.
"The last sentence is longer, but every part earns its place. It tells the reader exactly what was found and where."
Apply · 10 min
Students expand one kernel about their own opinion, using a fact from either text.
Session 5 · Building a reason paragraph

Paragraph frame: Point (one reason) · Evidence (a fact from a text, named) · Explain (why that fact supports the point) · Link (back to the opinion). Students build it from sentences they already wrote in Sessions 3–4.

Teacher model. One reason to bring beavers back is that their ponds can protect towns from floods. In "Welcome Back, Beavers?" the author describes a study in southwest England where beaver ponds held back large amounts of water, and during heavy rain the water flowed out more slowly. When water leaves slowly, rivers downstream are less likely to overflow all at once. That is a benefit for people, not just for wildlife, and it is a strong reason to let beavers return.

Students annotate the model, labeling each part, then write their own paragraph for a different reason.

Session 6 · Responding to the other side

Frame: Name the opposing reason fairly · Show you understand why someone holds it · Respond with evidence or a condition. Text A is useful here: a student who supports beavers must explain why Britain is different from Tierra del Fuego (beavers once lived in Britain; there are rules about where they can live).

Sentence starters: "Some people worry that ___. This concern makes sense because ___. However, ___." "It is true that in ___, ___. But Britain is different because ___."

Sessions 7–8 · Drafting and revising

Session 7: model an introduction that states the issue in one sentence and the opinion in the next; students draft the full essay from their planner. Session 8: students score their own draft with the rubric, then revise one trait; the teacher holds three-minute conferences focused on one trait per student.

6. Scored examples

{{LABEL:ill}} Both examples were written by me to show two score points. They are not student work.

Example 1 (total 9 of 20). I think beavers should come back to Britain. Beavers are good for the environment. They make ponds and wetlands. Frogs and birds live there. Some farmers dont like beavers. But beavers were there first. In conclusion beavers should come back.

Opinion and organization: 2Clear opinion, but the ideas are a list rather than grouped reasons; the conclusion repeats the opening.
Reasons and evidence: 2One general reason, loosely supported from Text B; no evidence from Text A.
Other side: 2Farmers are mentioned, and "were there first" is a response, but with no evidence.
Sentences and linking: 1Short sentences with no joining words; the session 3 and 4 work would help most here.
Conventions: 2"dont," missing comma after "In conclusion." Next step: combine sentences 2–4 into one sentence that joins the reason to its evidence.

Example 2 (total 19 of 20). Beavers disappeared from Britain hundreds of years ago, and now people are bringing them back. I believe this is a good idea, as long as there are rules to manage them.

The strongest reason is that beaver ponds can help prevent floods. In "Welcome Back, Beavers?" the author describes a study in southwest England where beaver ponds held back large amounts of water, so during heavy rain it flowed out more slowly. That could protect towns downstream. The same study found that the water leaving the site carried less soil and pollution, which is good for rivers and the people who use them.

Some people point to Tierra del Fuego, where beavers have destroyed forests. That is a fair worry. However, "When Engineers Cause Trouble" explains that the problem there is that the trees did not evolve with beavers and cannot recover the way North American trees can. Britain is different because beavers lived there for a very long time before they were hunted away.

Farmers' concerns are real, too, which is why rules about where beavers can live are important. If people plan carefully, Britain can get the benefits of beavers while protecting the land that people depend on.

ScoresOpinion and organization 4 · Reasons and evidence 4 · Other side 4 · Sentences and linking 4 · Conventions 3 (minor punctuation). Why it works: the opinion includes a condition ("as long as there are rules"), which the conclusion returns to; Text A is used to respond to the other side, not just to report facts. Next step: name a second, separate reason so the essay does not rest on the flood study alone.
