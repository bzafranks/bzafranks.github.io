1. Choosing the words

I sorted every candidate word in the five unit texts into three groups (Beck, McKeown & Kucan, 2013). Tier 2 words appear across many subjects and are worth deep instruction. Tier 3 words belong to this topic; they are taught briefly in context, in the lesson where they are needed. Everyday words need no instruction. A word made the Tier 2 list only if it appears in at least two unit texts or is central to the unit's big idea, and if students will meet it again in other subjects.

2. The routine for each Tier 2 word

Same steps every time, about four minutes per word, so students learn the routine once and then focus on the word.

  1. Say it. Teacher says the word; students repeat it; clap the syllables (de-pend, sur-vive).
  2. See it in the text. Read the sentence from the unit text aloud.
  3. Student-friendly definition. "If you depend on something, you need it in order to be okay."
  4. Examples and non-examples. "Does a baby depend on its parents? Does a rock depend on the rain?" Students answer with a thumbs signal, then explain one.
  5. Use it. Students complete a frame about their own lives: "I depend on ___ because ___."
  6. Connect it. Add the word to the class word wall under the unit's big idea.

3. Word parts that unlock other words

Four high-utility parts, chosen because they appear in the unit and in hundreds of other words students will meet (L.4.4b).

Sample morphology lesson: the root struct (20 minutes)
Hook · 3 min
Write struct on the board. "This word part comes from Latin and means build. Beavers are builders. Let's see how many words we can build from struct."
Teach · 5 min
Model with construct: con- (together) + struct (build) = to build by putting parts together. "A beaver constructs a dam." Then destruction: de- (down, undo) + struct + -ion (the act of). "Text 5 describes the destruction of a forest."
Practice · 8 min
Word sort in pairs: structure, construct, instruct, destruction, strict, construction. Students sort into "has the root struct" and "doesn't." Strict is the trap: it looks similar but does not contain struct.
Apply · 4 min
"Write one sentence about an ecosystem engineer using a struct word." Share two aloud.

4. Practice with corrective feedback

Practice matters only if errors are caught and fixed right away. These scripts give teachers specific language for the errors students most often make.

ActivityLikely errorTeacher response
Which word fits? "The forest cannot ___ after the flood." (recover / restore)Chooses restore"Who is doing the action in this sentence? The forest. Can a forest restore itself, or do people restore it? Recover is what the forest does; restore is what people do. Try the sentence again."
Depend or rely: explain the difference"They mean exactly the same.""They are close. Listen: 'I rely on my alarm clock.' 'Plants depend on sunlight.' Which one could you live without if you had to?" Guide to: depend often means you truly need it; rely means you trust it to be there.
Use un-: what does unwelcome mean?"Very welcome""Cover un-. What's left? Welcome. Now add back un-, which means not. So unwelcome means…?"
Does strict contain the root struct?"Yes""Look letter by letter: s-t-r-i-c-t. Our root is s-t-r-u-c-t. Close, but not the same. And does strict mean anything about building? Good readers check both the spelling and the meaning."
Use benefit in a sentence"The beaver benefit the pond.""Who gets the help in your sentence? The beaver gives help; the animals get it. Try: 'The animals benefit from…'"

6. Checking progress

A short check at the end of the unit, separate from the WS6 assessment: for each Tier 2 word, students (1) choose the sentence that uses it correctly from three, and (2) write their own sentence. For word parts, they explain the meaning of two words they were never taught (for example, unsafe and reconstruct) and name the parts that helped. The second task is the real test: can they use the parts on a new word?
