1. The rubric

One rubric for all three artifacts, so scores can be compared. Each criterion is scored 1–4. Release gate: any score of 2 or lower on Accuracy blocks release regardless of the total, because students learn errors as facts.

Evaluator write-up

This deck is the strongest of the three on content and the weakest on delivery. The core of the lesson is good: slide 4 gives two short, believable accounts of the same event, and slide 5's table models exactly the comparison RI.5.6 asks for, separating shared facts from differences in focus, tone and purpose. A teacher could teach from these two slides as they are.

The problems are in the frame around that core. First, the guided practice on slide 6 drops back to two single sentences about a garden, so students practice spotting opinion words but never compare two full accounts with support. I would replace slide 6 with a third short pair of accounts and have the class fill in the slide 5 table together. Second, the layout has visible defects a teacher would notice in front of the class: the slide 5 footer overlaps the callout, every footer runs into its page number, and the numbering is off by one. Third, legibility: the footer contrast is about 4.27:1, and much of the card text is 18–20 px, which is hard to read from the back of a room. I would raise body text to at least 24 px and remove the low-contrast footers. Fourth, the exit ticket's second question needs to name which accounts it means, and the teacher's answer notes on slide 8 belong in speaker notes, not on the projected slide. Finally, color: the green and red cards on slide 3 hint at the answer before students read, which undercuts the point of looking for word clues. Use one neutral color for both. With these changes, I would expect this deck to score 3 or 4 on every criterion.

Evaluator write-up

This artifact shows why a total score is not enough. It reads smoothly, its structure is clear, and it scores one point higher than the slide deck. It also teaches a misconception. Saying that hail falls "when the air is very cold" puts hail next to snow and sleet as a cold-weather event, when it forms in strong thunderstorm updrafts and often falls in summer. A Grade 4 student would remember this as a fact. Under the release gate, that one sentence blocks the artifact.

The other accuracy problems are the kind AI output often has: claims stated with more confidence than they deserve. The "approximately 830L" Lexile is a guess presented as a measure; only the Lexile Analyzer produces a Lexile. The band it cites is real but from the wrong appendix. The Q5 answer key accepts "first," a word that is not in the passage, so a scorer could credit an answer the text does not support.

The items need the most work. Q1 has one plausible answer, so it measures test-taking, not main idea. Q2 asks students to define "condensation" when the passage defines it in bold in the previous sentence. I would revise Q1 with distractors that are each true details but not the main idea, replace Q2 with a word students must infer from context, give Q3 four real steps, and correct the Q5 key. With those changes and the three sentence-level fixes below, this becomes a usable passage. My revised version follows.

Evaluator write-up

This is the most usable of the three artifacts, and its strengths are real. The praise is specific: it quotes the student's own details (meeting Maya, the ball bin) rather than saying "good job." Every error it flags is genuine, and each correction is shown in context. The tone is one a student would want to read.

Its main weakness is calibration. Scoring conventions 3 out of 4 for an essay with four repeated homophone errors tells the student their conventions are nearly there when they are a clear area for growth. Inflated scores are a common pattern in AI-written feedback, and they matter because students and families read the numbers first. I would score conventions 2 and organization 3, and keep the rest. The feedback also contradicts itself on the exercise reason, praising the "wiggly" detail as strong evidence and then saying the reason needs more support. A teacher should resolve that before sending it.

Second, there is too much of it. Three next steps and a four-part error list is a lot for one revision. I would keep one content next step (explain the exercise reason) and one conventions focus (its/it's), and save the rest. I would also remove the model sentence; giving students a sentence to paste in teaches them to copy, not to elaborate. Finally, the feedback should be anchored to a rubric the class already knows, with descriptors, so that "3 out of 4" means something the student can look up.

5. Patterns across the three

  • Confident precision without a source: a Lexile number, a standard's appendix, an answer key word. Each looks authoritative and is wrong. Every number and citation in AI output needs checking against its source.
  • The parts do not fully line up: guided practice that skips the main skill, an exit ticket that names the wrong accounts, feedback that contradicts itself. Reviewers should check each part against the others, not only against the prompt.
  • Polish hides defects: the deck looks professional and still has overlapping text and low-contrast footers. Review rendered output, not just the source.
