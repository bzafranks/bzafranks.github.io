Lesson 1 · 10 minutes

Why feedback conversations stall

By the end of this lesson you can recognize three patterns that turn a feedback conversation into a one-way report.

Start from your own experience

Three patterns

Most stalled conversations show at least one of these:

  1. The verdict. The mentor opens with a judgment ("That lesson was a bit chaotic"). The teacher defends instead of thinking.
  2. The list. The mentor names everything noticed. The teacher leaves with eight points and acts on none.
  3. The monologue. The mentor does almost all the talking. The teacher agrees politely, and the plan belongs to the mentor.

Each pattern has the same cost: the teacher does not do the thinking, so the change does not stick.

Which pattern do you fall into most? Open for a quick self-check.

If you often run out of time, you may be giving lists. If teachers often say "yes, I know," you may be giving verdicts. If you rarely hear the teacher's own idea for a next step, you may be giving monologues. Most of us do all three at times.

Lesson 2 · 15 minutes

The four moves

By the end of this lesson you can name the four moves of a specific, two-way feedback conversation and spot each one in a real exchange.

MoveWhat you doSounds like
1. EvidenceDescribe one thing you saw or heard, without judging it."During the partner talk, I counted four pairs who were talking about the weekend."
2. EffectConnect it to what students learned or did."Those pairs didn't have an answer to share when you called the class back."
3. QuestionAsk a real question and wait. Let the teacher analyze it."What do you think was going on for those pairs?"
4. Next stepAgree on one small, specific action and when you will look for it."So you'll give each pair a sentence starter. I'll stop by Thursday's partner talk."

One focus per conversation. If you saw five things, choose the one that will make the biggest difference for students this week.

Worked example

Read the conversation. The notes on the right show which move is happening and why it works. Illustrative dialogue

ConversationWhat the mentor is doing
Mentor: Thanks for having me in. I want to focus on one part, the partner talk after the read-aloud. Is that all right?Names a single focus at the start and asks permission. This prevents a list.
Teacher: Sure. I felt like it went okay.
Mentor: I wrote down what I heard from six pairs near me. Two were talking about the text. Four were talking about the weekend.Evidence. A count, not an opinion. Hard to argue with, easy to think about.
Mentor: When you called the class back, those four pairs didn't have an answer ready.Effect. Links the behavior to learning, which is what the teacher cares about.
Mentor: What do you think was going on for them?Question. Open, genuine, and followed by silence. The mentor does not answer it.
Teacher: Maybe the question was too big? "What did you think of the story" doesn't really give them a place to start.The teacher does the analysis. This is the moment the change becomes theirs.
Mentor: What could give them a place to start?Stays in question mode instead of supplying the fix.
Teacher: I could put a sentence starter on the board. Like "The character changed when…"
Mentor: Good. Let's try that on Thursday, and I'll come by for the partner talk and count again.Next step. One action, a date, and a way to check it.

Lesson 3 · 12 minutes

Practice: a conversation with Ms. Rolle

By the end of this lesson you can choose mentor responses that keep a conversation specific and two-way, and explain why.

Ms. Rolle is in her second year teaching Grade 4. You observed a 20-minute reading lesson. During independent reading, three students at the back table read for about four minutes, then stopped and spent the rest of the time sharpening pencils and drawing. Ms. Rolle was working with a small group the whole time and did not look up. Fictional scenario

Knowledge check · 5 questions

Check your understanding

Choose an answer for each question, then select Check answers. You can try again as often as you like.

1. Which opening is most likely to keep the conversation two-way?

2. Which is the best example of the Evidence move?

3. You observed five things worth discussing. What should you do?

4. After you ask a question, the teacher is silent. What should you do?

5. Which next step is strongest?
