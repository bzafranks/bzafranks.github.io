Look at this first: "The Crunching Sound". Its readability formula says Grade 2. The qualitative analysis explains why it belongs in Grade 4, which is the kind of judgment a formula cannot make.

How I placed each text

I used the three-part model from the Common Core's Appendix A: quantitative measures, qualitative features, and the reader and task. For the quantitative measure I report a Flesch–Kincaid grade estimate, computed with an automated syllable count, and compare it with the grade-band ranges published in the Supplemental Information for Appendix A (2012): about 1.98–5.34 for Grades 2–3 and 4.51–7.73 for Grades 4–5. Formulas only see sentence and word length, so they are a starting point. Where my qualitative judgment differs from the formula, I say why.

Text 1 · Grade 3 · Literary

{{INCLUDE:text-bigclaw}}
QuantitativeFK 2.7, inside the Grades 2–3 range. Short sentences, mostly one- and two-syllable words.
QualitativeMeaning: one clear theme (sharing what you worked for), shown through a character change rather than stated. Structure: chronological, with a classic problem and turn. Language: some figurative language ("the color of an old bruise," "baked in the sun") that is supported by context. Knowledge: local animals and plants (land crab, sea grapes, curly-tailed lizard) that Bahamian readers know and others can picture.
Reader and taskGood for discussing how a character changes (RL.3.3) and for a read-aloud that previews "burrow" and "shelter" before the Grade 4 unit. The last line rewards inference: why does she dig wider?
PlacementGrade 3. Formula and qualitative judgment agree.

Text 2 · Grade 3 · Informational

{{INCLUDE:text-tunnels}}
QuantitativeFK 2.9, inside the Grades 2–3 range.
QualitativePurpose: explicit, stated in the first paragraph. Structure: a simple explanation with signal words ("First," "Second," "also," "too"). Language: two domain words, "casting" and "nutrients," each defined in the same sentence. Knowledge: familiar topic; one simile ("like stirring a pot of soup") supports the hardest idea.
Reader and taskSuited to RI.3.8 (connections between sentences and paragraphs) and RI.3.3 (cause and effect). Works as the first text in a Grade 3 version of the strand.
PlacementGrade 3. Formula and qualitative judgment agree.

Text 3 · Grade 4 · Informational

{{INCLUDE:text-dam}}
QuantitativeFK 4.7, at the low end of the Grades 4–5 range.
QualitativePurpose: explicit, but the central concept (ecosystem engineer) is held back until paragraph 5, so readers must connect the earlier examples to the term. Structure: cause and effect, chained across paragraphs (dam, pond, warm water, insects, fish, birds, plants). Language: domain words "predators," "lodge," "wetland," "habitat," "ecosystem engineers," mostly defined in context; one metaphor ("the pond is a fortress"). Knowledge: assumes readers know what a stream and a forest are; no prior science needed.
Reader and taskThe cause-and-effect chain makes it the right anchor for RI.4.3 (explain what happened and why). Placed first in the unit so every later text can be compared with it.
PlacementGrade 4. The formula is at the low end, but the delayed concept and the multi-step causal chain raise the qualitative demand.

Text 4 · Grade 4 · Literary

{{INCLUDE:text-crunching}}
QuantitativeFK 1.8, below the Grades 4–5 range. Dialogue makes sentences very short, which pulls the formula down.
QualitativeMeaning: the key event is never stated. Readers must infer what the "small white cloud" is from Kiara's reaction and her uncle's laugh, then follow her change from wanting to stop the fish to wanting to thank it. Structure: a single scene with a misunderstanding and a reversal. Language: sensory and figurative ("spread out like a garden," onomatopoeia); an unfinished sentence ("So the beach...") that asks the reader to complete the thought. Knowledge: embeds the same science as the informational text "The Sand Makers" (WS1), so the pair supports comparing how literary and informational texts present one topic.
Reader and taskRL.4.3 (describe a character using specific details) and RI.4.9-style comparison with the paired informational text.
PlacementGrade 4, above the formula. The inference load, implied humor and the paired-text task make it a Grade 4 text despite short sentences. This is exactly where a single readability score would misplace a text.

Text 5 · Grade 5 · Informational

{{INCLUDE:text-trouble}}
QuantitativeFK 7.4, at the high end of the Grades 4–5 range.
QualitativePurpose: complicates the unit's earlier message; readers must hold two ideas at once (engineers help; engineers harm). Structure: claim, extended example, explanation of cause, conclusion that returns to the claim. Language: academic phrasing ("did not evolve with," "restore," "costly"), a place name, and a concluding quotation that summarizes the argument. Knowledge: depends on knowing how beavers change land from Text 3; without it, the text is much harder.
Reader and taskRI.5.8 (how an author uses reasons and evidence) and RI.5.9 (integrate several texts). In Grade 4, it is read last, with support, after students know beavers well.
PlacementGrade 5, and a Grade 4 stretch text at the end of the unit. Knowledge built by Texts 3 and WS1's other texts is what makes it reachable.

Text 6 · Grade 5 · Literary

{{INCLUDE:text-reef}}
QuantitativeFK 3.5, below the Grades 4–5 range.
QualitativeMeaning: theme of patience shown, not told; Malik's final line signals a change the reader must interpret. Structure: a time shift carried by a photograph (last spring versus now) that readers must track. Language: longer, layered sentences in narration ("and, he soon discovered, maddening"), precise verbs, and some technical vocabulary (nursery, fragment, current). Knowledge: coral restoration may be unfamiliar; enough is explained in dialogue to follow it.
Reader and taskRL.5.2 (theme from how a character responds to a challenge) and RL.5.3 (compare characters). Also the source for the community adaptation in WS11.
PlacementGrade 5, above the formula, because of the time shift, the implied theme and the layered narration.
