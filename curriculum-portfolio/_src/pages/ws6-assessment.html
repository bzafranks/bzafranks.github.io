WS6 · Assessment design

Measuring What the Unit Teaches

An end-of-unit assessment for Nature's Engineers. A new text tests transfer; items on the unit texts test knowledge built across the unit. Every item is mapped to a standard, and every wrong answer is designed to show a specific misunderstanding.

Role
Assessment writer
Items
22: 15 multiple choice, 3 two-part, 2 multi-select, 2 written
Points
31
Time
About 60 minutes, one sitting or two
Texts
1 new (cold read) + 4 unit texts

2. Part A: new text

Students read this text for the first time during the assessment.

{{INCLUDE:text-elephants}}

264 words · FK estimate 5.6 · Grade 4. Chosen as a transfer text: a new animal, but the same cause-and-effect structure and the same "help or harm" idea as the unit.

  1. What is the main idea of the text? (RI.4.2)
    A. Elephants need more water than other animals in Africa.
    B. Elephants change their environment in ways that can help other animals survive, though they can also cause harm.
    C. Many animals in Africa drink from holes that elephants dig.
    D. Too many elephants in one area can damage a woodland.
  2. Part A: Why do other animals depend on elephants' holes in the dry season? (RI.4.3)
    A. The holes are cooler than the riverbed.
    B. Many of the animals cannot dig deep enough to reach the water.
    C. Elephants share their water with other animals.
    D. The holes fill with rain every night.
    Part B: Which sentence from the text best supports your answer to Part A?
    A. "Then they drink, using their trunks like straws."
    B. "When the elephants move on, the holes stay behind."
    C. "Many of them cannot dig deep enough to reach the water."
    D. "Grazing animals then feed on the grass."
  3. Which of these happens first? (RI.4.3)
    A. Zebras and birds drink from the hole.
    B. Water seeps into the bottom of the hole.
    C. The river dries up, leaving water below the sand.
    D. The elephants move on.
  4. In paragraph 2, "water seeps into the bottom of the hole." What does seeps mean? (RI.4.4)
    A. splashes in quickly
    B. flows in slowly, a little at a time
    C. dries up completely
    D. turns into ice
  5. Choose two ways the text says elephants change their habitat besides digging for water. (RI.4.1)
    A. They push over trees, which makes space for grass.
    B. They plant new trees with seeds they carry.
    C. Their footprints can hold rainwater where tiny animals live.
    D. They fill dry rivers with water from their trunks.
    E. They dig tunnels where birds build nests.
  6. Which detail supports the author's point that "not every change an elephant makes is welcome"? (RI.4.8)
    A. Elephants use their trunks like straws.
    B. Too many elephants can knock down so many trees that a woodland is damaged.
    C. Zebras, antelope, baboons and birds visit the holes.
    D. Elephants dig with their feet and trunks.
  7. How are paragraphs 1–3 organized? (RI.4.5)
    A. A problem is described, and then a solution.
    B. Two animals are compared.
    C. Events are listed in the order they happened over many years.
    D. An opinion is given with reasons.
  8. According to paragraph 4, how does pushing over trees help grazing animals? (RI.4.3)
    A. The fallen trees give them shade.
    B. It opens space for the grass they eat.
    C. It frightens away predators.
    D. The trees fall into the river and make ponds.
  9. In paragraph 1, the author says one animal "knows a secret." What is the secret? (RI.4.4)
    A. Where the coolest places are in the dry season
    B. That water is hidden under the sand of a dry river
    C. How to find other elephants
    D. That rain is coming soon
  10. Which is the best summary of paragraph 5? (RI.4.2)
    A. Elephants always help their environment.
    B. Elephants can harm a woodland when there are too many of them, so whether they help or harm depends on the situation.
    C. Elephants knock down trees.
    D. Ecosystem engineers live in many places.
  11. Why does the author end the text with "a hole dug by an elephant can save the lives of the animals that come after it"? (RI.4.1)
    A. To show that elephants are dangerous
    B. To remind readers how important the holes are, even after describing the harm elephants can do
    C. To explain how elephants dig
    D. To tell readers that the dry season is ending
  12. Which sentence best explains why an elephant can be called an ecosystem engineer? (RI.4.3)
    A. Elephants are very large and strong.
    B. Elephants change their habitat in ways that other living things come to depend on.
    C. Elephants live in parts of Africa where it does not rain for months.
    D. Elephants eat leaves and bark.

3. Part B: unit texts

Students may look back at the unit texts.

  1. According to "One Dam, Many Neighbors" and "Builders Underground," how are beavers and gopher tortoises alike? (RI.4.9)
    A. Both live mostly in water.
    B. Both build something that other animals use for shelter or a home.
    C. Both are keystone species found only in Florida.
    D. Both cut down trees.
  2. Part A: How is the elephant in "Diggers of the Dry Season" like the beaver in "One Dam, Many Neighbors"? (RI.4.9)
    A. Both build homes of sticks and mud.
    B. Both change where water is found, and other animals use it.
    C. Both are hunted by bears.
    D. Both live in Africa.
    Part B: Choose one sentence from each text that supports your answer.
    From "Diggers of the Dry Season": A. "Zebras, antelope, baboons and birds visit them to drink." B. "They push over trees to eat leaves and bark."
    From "One Dam, Many Neighbors": C. "The water backs up behind the dam and spreads out into a pond." D. "They cut down small trees with their strong front teeth."
  3. In "The Sand Makers," what happens to the pieces of rock and coral a parrotfish swallows? (RI.4.3)
    A. They stay in its stomach to help it sink.
    B. They are ground into fine grains and pass out of its body as sand.
    C. They are spit back onto the reef to build new coral.
    D. They are turned into algae.
  4. Part A: In "The Crunching Sound," how does Kiara's thinking about the parrotfish change? (RL.4.3)
    A. She is afraid of it at first, then she wants to touch it.
    B. She thinks it is harming the reef at first, then she is thankful for it.
    C. She does not notice it at first, then she wants to catch it.
    D. She likes it at first, then she wants to leave.
    Part B: Which two details show this change?
    A. "It's wrecking the reef," Kiara said. "Can't we stop it?"
    B. Her uncle swam over and pushed his mask up.
    C. This time she did not want to stop it. She wanted to say thank you.
    D. Kiara floated face down in the clear water off Taino Beach.
  5. In "When Engineers Cause Trouble," why do beavers harm the forests of Tierra del Fuego more than forests in North America? (RI.4.8)
    A. The beavers there are larger.
    B. Many trees there cannot recover after beavers cut them down.
    C. The rivers there are faster.
    D. People there hunt beavers more.
  6. Which two statements are supported by "One Dam, Many Neighbors," "When Engineers Cause Trouble," and "Diggers of the Dry Season" together? (RI.4.9)
    A. An ecosystem engineer can help or harm, depending on the place.
    B. All ecosystem engineers live near water.
    C. Ecosystem engineers change their habitats in ways that affect other living things.
    D. Ecosystem engineers are always welcome.
    E. Only large animals can be ecosystem engineers.

    C. sounds and echoes

5. Part D: written responses

  1. Short response (2 points). Explain how a hole dug by an elephant in a dry riverbed helps other animals. Use two details from "Diggers of the Dry Season." (RI.4.3)
  2. Extended response (4 points). Using "Diggers of the Dry Season" and one other text from the unit, explain how both animals are ecosystem engineers. Include details from both texts. (RI.4.9, W.4.9b)

6. Answer key and distractor rationales

ItemKeyWhat each wrong answer suggests
1BA: plausible but not stated (reading in outside ideas). C: a supporting detail, not the main idea (detail versus main idea). D: a detail from one paragraph (overweighting the end of the text).
2A: B · B: CA: invented reason. C: misreads purpose (elephants do not intend to share). D: unsupported. Part B, A and B: true but do not explain dependence; D: a different effect.
3CA, B, D: later steps. Choosing B often means the student started at the elephant's action, not the condition that causes it.
4BA: ignores "seeps" versus "splashes." C, D: opposite or unrelated; suggests guessing without context.
5A, CB, D, E: invented actions; a student who picks these is relying on memory of other animals (E echoes the gopher tortoise).
6BA, C, D: true details that support other points; tests matching evidence to a specific claim.
7AB: confuses with the unit's comparison lessons. C: chronology is partly present but the organizing logic is problem and solution. D: not an opinion text.
8BA, C: plausible but unstated. D: transfers the beaver's effect to elephants (mixing up texts).
9BA, C, D: plausible meanings of "secret" that ignore the next paragraph, which explains it.
10BA: the opposite of the paragraph. C: too narrow. D: too broad.
11BA: contradicts the sentence. C: confuses topic with purpose. D: unsupported.
12BA, C, D: true facts that do not define the concept; tests whether the student holds the unit's core idea.
13BA: true only of beavers. C: overgeneralizes (and "only in Florida" is not stated). D: true only of beavers.
14A: B · B: A and CA: beaver only. C: not stated for elephants. D: elephant only. Part B, B and D: true sentences about each animal, but not about water.
15BA, C, D: invented processes; D confuses what the fish eats with what it makes.
16A: B · B: A and CA, C, D: misread Kiara's starting feeling. Part B, B and D: true details that do not show change.
17BA, C, D: plausible but not the reason the text gives.
18A, CB: overgeneralizes (tortoises and elephants). D: contradicted by all three. E: contradicted by parrotfish and the unit as a whole.
19BC: sound-alike ("echo"); a decoding confusion. A, D: unrelated.
20BA: the most common everyday meaning, ignoring context. C: the opposite of "crowd out." D: unrelated.

Two-part items: 2 points if both parts are correct, 1 point if Part A only is correct. Multi-select items: 2 points for both correct choices and no incorrect ones; 1 point for one correct choice and no incorrect ones.

PointsDescription

Scored sample at 3 points {{LABEL:ill}}: "Elephants and beavers are both ecosystem engineers. Elephants dig holes in dry rivers to get water, and then zebras and birds drink from them. Beavers build dams that make ponds, and frogs, ducks and fish live in the pond. Both animals change their homes and help other animals." Why 3, not 4: both animals and both texts are used accurately, but the response does not explain why that makes them engineers (other animals come to depend on the change).

8. Item review checklist

Every item was checked against this list before inclusion.

  • Measures the standard it is mapped to, and only that standard where possible.
  • Answerable from the text; no outside knowledge required.
  • One defensible key; every distractor is plausible and clearly wrong for a stated reason.
  • Options are similar in length and grammar, so the key does not stand out.
  • No "all of the above," "none of the above," or negative stems.
  • Location references (paragraph numbers, titles) match the text exactly.
  • Vocabulary items test words not pre-taught in the unit.
  • Language is plain enough that reading the question is not harder than the skill tested.
  • No bias: names, settings and topics are fair to all students.

One revision from that review: item 20 originally tested "fortress" from Text 1. That word is taught in Lesson 2, so the item would have measured recall of a lesson, not use of context. It was replaced with "compete," which is not pre-taught and has a clear clue in the next sentence.
