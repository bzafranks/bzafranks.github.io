One Dam, Many Neighbors

Original text by Barbara Zá Franks · 321 words

1A stream runs through a forest. Its water is shallow and quick. A few fish dart under the rocks, and birds stop to drink at the edge. Then a family of beavers arrives, and over the next few years, almost everything about this place will change.

2Beavers are builders. They cut down small trees with their strong front teeth. They drag the branches into the stream and pack them together with mud and stones. Bit by bit, they build a dam, a wall that blocks the flowing water. The water backs up behind the dam and spreads out into a pond.

3For the beavers, the pond is a fortress. Deep water keeps them safe from predators such as coyotes and bears, which cannot easily follow them underwater. In the middle of the pond, the beavers build a lodge of sticks and mud. Its doors are underwater, so the family can come and go without ever stepping onto land.

4But the beavers are not the only ones who benefit. The slow, still water of the pond warms in the sun. Insects lay their eggs in it, and fish and frogs come to eat the insects. Ducks and herons arrive to eat the fish and frogs. Around the edges, the ground stays soggy, and new plants take root. The quick little stream has become a wetland, one of the busiest kinds of habitat on Earth.

5Scientists have a name for animals that change a habitat so much that other living things depend on the change. They call them ecosystem engineers. The beaver is one of the best-known examples. One dam can turn a quiet stream into a home for many kinds of plants and animals, from tiny water beetles to moose.

6The next time you see a pond in the woods, look closely at its edge. You might find a wall of sticks and mud, and the work of a family of engineers.
