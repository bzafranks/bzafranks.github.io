When Engineers Cause Trouble

Original text by Barbara Zá Franks · 293 words

1Ecosystem engineers are often described as heroes. Beavers create wetlands, gopher tortoises shelter hundreds of species, and parrotfish help keep coral reefs healthy. But an engineer's effects depend on where it is working. In the wrong place, the same building skills can cause serious harm.

2Consider Tierra del Fuego, a group of islands at the southern tip of South America. In the 1940s, a small number of North American beavers were brought there in the hope of starting a fur trade. The fur business never succeeded, but the beavers did. With few predators to hunt them, their numbers grew, and they spread from island to island. Today tens of thousands of beavers live there.

3The forests of Tierra del Fuego did not evolve with beavers. In North America, many trees along streams can sprout again after a beaver cuts them down. Many of the trees in Tierra del Fuego cannot recover the same way. Where beavers have built dams, whole stretches of forest have flooded and died, leaving gray, standing trunks. Scientists who study the region describe it as one of the largest changes to these forests in thousands of years.

4Argentina and Chile, the two countries that share Tierra del Fuego, have worked together on plans to remove the beavers and restore the damaged land. It is a difficult and costly job. It shows that an animal's effect on an environment is not simply good or bad. It depends on the animal, the place, and the other living things that share it.

5The same beaver that builds a home for hundreds of species in one place can tear a forest apart in another. When scientists ask whether an ecosystem engineer is helpful or harmful, the honest answer usually begins with: "It depends."
