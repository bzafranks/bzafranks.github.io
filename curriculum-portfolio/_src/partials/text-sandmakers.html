The Sand Makers

Original text by Barbara Zá Franks · 244 words

1Walk along a beach in The Bahamas and scoop up a handful of soft white sand. Some of the sand in your hand may have passed through the body of a fish.

2That fish is the parrotfish. Parrotfish live on coral reefs, and they are easy to spot. Many are bright blue, green or pink, and their teeth are joined together into a hard beak, like a parrot's. Parrotfish spend most of the day eating. They use their beaks to scrape algae off coral and rock. Algae are tiny plant-like living things that grow on reefs.

3When a parrotfish scrapes off algae, it also bites off small pieces of the hard rock and coral underneath. Inside its throat, special teeth grind those pieces into fine grains. The parrotfish digests the algae, and the ground-up rock passes out of its body as sand. A single large parrotfish can make a surprising amount of sand over a year.

4Parrotfish help the reef in another way, too. Coral and algae compete for space. If algae grows too thick, it can crowd out young corals and block the sunlight they need. By eating algae all day long, parrotfish help keep space open for corals to grow.

5So parrotfish are engineers twice over. They help keep the reef healthy, and they help build the beaches where people swim and sea turtles lay their eggs. The next time you dig your toes into Bahamian sand, you can thank a fish.
