Welcome Back, Beavers?

Original text by Barbara Zá Franks · 282 words

1Hundreds of years ago, the last wild beavers in Britain disappeared. People had hunted them until none were left. For centuries, Britain's rivers flowed without a single beaver dam.

2Today, beavers are returning. In 2009, a trial began in Scotland in which a small number of beavers were released into the wild and carefully studied. Other beavers have been released in England, some into fenced areas where scientists can watch what happens.

3Supporters of bringing beavers back point to what the scientists have found. In one study in southwest England, researchers measured the water flowing out of a fenced site where beavers had built a series of dams. The ponds held back large amounts of water. During heavy rain, water flowed out of the site more slowly, which could help reduce flooding in towns downstream. The water leaving the site also carried less soil and pollution than the water flowing in. Supporters also say the new wetlands give homes to insects, frogs, birds and other wildlife.

4Not everyone is pleased. Some farmers worry that beaver dams will flood their fields or that beavers will gnaw down valuable trees. Some people who fish are concerned that dams could block fish that swim upstream. Managing beavers, such as moving a dam or protecting trees, takes time and money, and people disagree about who should pay.

5Because of these concerns, the return of beavers in Britain has come with rules about where they can live and what landowners may do when beavers cause problems. Bringing back an animal that has been gone for hundreds of years, it turns out, is not just a question for scientists. It is a question for everyone who shares the land.
