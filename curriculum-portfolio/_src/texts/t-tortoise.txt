Builders Underground

In the sandy pine forests of the southeastern United States, there is a builder you might walk right past. The gopher tortoise is a slow, shy reptile with a high, domed shell and front legs shaped like flat shovels. It spends much of its life underground, in a burrow it digs itself.

A gopher tortoise burrow is no small hole. It slopes down into the sand, often about fifteen feet long, and some are much longer. At the bottom, the air stays cool on scorching summer days and mild on cold nights. The tortoise uses its burrow to escape heat, cold and predators.

The tortoise does not keep its burrow to itself. Scientists have found hundreds of other kinds of animals using gopher tortoise burrows. Frogs hide there during the day. Snakes, mice and insects move in. Some animals, such as the gopher frog, depend so much on the burrows that they are hard to find anywhere else. When a forest fire sweeps through, many animals survive by crowding into a burrow until the danger passes.

Because so many animals rely on its burrows, the gopher tortoise is called a keystone species. In a stone arch, the keystone is the piece at the top that holds the other stones in place. Take it away, and the arch falls. Take the gopher tortoise away, and many of the animals that use its burrows would struggle to survive.

Like the beaver, the gopher tortoise changes its habitat in a way that helps others. The beaver does it with water. The tortoise does it with sand.
