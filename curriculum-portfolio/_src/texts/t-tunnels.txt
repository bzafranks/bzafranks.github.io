Tunnels Under the Garden

Dig into a healthy garden and you will probably find an earthworm. It may not look like much. It has no eyes, no legs and no bones. But an earthworm is a hard worker, and it changes the soil in ways that help plants grow.

Earthworms make tunnels. A worm pushes through the soil by stretching its body forward and then pulling the back end along. As it moves, it leaves a tunnel behind it. Some worms stay near the top of the soil. Others dig deep tunnels that go straight down.

These tunnels help the soil in two ways. First, they let air in. Plant roots need air, just like we do. Second, the tunnels let rain soak down into the ground instead of running off the top. Water can then reach the roots that need it.

Earthworms also eat as they dig. They swallow bits of soil and dead leaves. Their bodies break the leaves down. What comes out the other end is called a casting. Castings are full of nutrients, the things plants need to grow strong.

Earthworms mix the soil, too. They pull leaves to the openings of their tunnels. They bring soil from deep below up to the top. Over time, this mixing spreads nutrients through the soil, a little like stirring a pot of soup.

So the next time you see an earthworm on the sidewalk after a rain, think about the work it does. Under every garden is a busy world of tunnels, and earthworms are its builders.
