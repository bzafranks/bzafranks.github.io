Beavers are some of the hardest-working animals in North America. Scientists call them ecosystem engineers because what they build can change the land around them for many years.

A beaver's most famous project is the dam. Beavers build dams across streams and creeks. They gnaw down small trees with their strong, orange front teeth. Then they drag branches, mud and stones into the water and pack them tightly together. Over time, the dam grows taller and wider, and the water behind it rises into a pond.

Why do beavers go to all this trouble? A deep pond is a safer place to live. Water helps protect beavers from predators such as wolves, coyotes and bears. The pond is also a pantry. Beavers do not hibernate, so in autumn they push branches into the mud at the bottom of the pond. In winter, they swim under the ice to eat them.

In the pond, beavers build a lodge, a dome-shaped home of sticks and mud. Its entrances are underwater, so the family can swim in and out safely. Inside, a dry space above the water is where the beavers sleep and raise their young.

The changes beavers make do not stop with their own homes. As the pond spreads, it can create a wetland. Cattails and water lilies grow in the shallow, soggy soil. Frogs, ducks, fish and turtles move in to feed and hide. Dragonflies lay their eggs in the still water, and herons hunt for fish.

Beaver ponds can cause problems, too. Sometimes they flood roads or fields, and people have to manage them. But scientists have found that beaver ponds also help the land. They slow down rushing water, which can reduce flooding downstream. They store water for dry months, and they trap mud before it reaches rivers. In this way, one beaver family can transform a quiet stream into a busy home for many living things.
